Power Trial Functions for Landscape-Based Spectral Bounds

A stretched string settles into a resting shape. How much of its sound can that quiet shape alone reveal?

Strike a string and it sounds a sequence of vibrational modes; leave it under a steady load and it simply sags into a profile. This paper asks how much about the first can be recovered from the second, for a one-dimensional Schrödinger operator with Dirichlet boundary conditions.

The resting profile turns out to be a complete fingerprint of the operator: it determines the potential exactly. It also generates a simple family of trial states, built by raising the profile to a power, that yields sharper estimates of the lowest vibrational energy than the standard landscape bound. On the free interval, the best member of the family brings the estimate from 10 down to about 9.90, against an exact value of about 9.87.

The study separates three ideas that are easily conflated: the landscape can identify the coefficient, its reciprocal can describe where waves localize, and nonlinear functions of the landscape can serve as better variational trial states. The power family improves the standard bound whenever the exponent is taken slightly above one, for any nonconstant positive landscape.

Restricting to a small two-term trial space gives a further explicit bound, and numerical experiments test how accurately the potential can be reconstructed and how sensitive that reconstruction is to small perturbations of the landscape. The one-dimensional setting is deliberate: it keeps every quantity analytically tractable.

On the Spectral Properties of Discrete Landscape Functions

Can the shape of a simple path tell us something exact about the waves it can carry?

We derive an exact formula for a discrete landscape and study how it breaks into spectral modes—the basic patterns of vibration on the interval. Each mode has a coefficient, and those coefficients turn out to have surprisingly rich arithmetic structure. For the first mode, every tested interval from \(N=3\) through \(N=30\) reached the strongest complexity allowed by our bound. That recurring pattern became the Chandra–Jain Conjecture.

The conjecture

Does this pattern hold for every interval length?

The conjecture in this preprint was later proved by Q. Zhou.

A landscape function turns an operator problem into a positive function one can inspect. Here, the discretized interval gives an explicit profile; projecting that profile onto spectral modes produces coefficients whose exact values carry arithmetic information.

The paper bounds the field degree of every odd mode by \(\varphi(N)\). Computation for the first mode suggested that this upper bound is always attained. The paper follows that conjecture through reductions to coprime cases and explores links to parity, the Arnold cat map, and Lefschetz numbers. The conjecture is now proved in the separate Zenodo note linked in the Read tab.

Preprint · Paper IIJuly 2026

Arithmetic Landscape Functions of a Discrete Cat Map

A deterministic map shuffles points on a finite grid. Its Green function remembers the exact length of every point’s orbit.

Imagine tracing a point under the cat map: it jumps around a finite torus and eventually returns to where it started. The diagonal Green function of \(L_N=I-\alpha P\) has the exact value \((1-\alpha^{k_x})^{-1}\), where \(k_x\) is that point’s shortest return time. In this setting, a spectral landscape becomes a map of the system’s periodic orbits.

The origin is the unique fixed point and the unique maximum. The localization comes from arithmetic structure alone. Further results connect the Green trace to the dynamical zeta function and show how adding a Laplacian weakens the localization gap.

Because the map acts on a finite set, every orbit is periodic. The operator’s inverse sums the repeated action of the map, so its diagonal records how quickly each point returns to itself. The exact closed form makes that relationship visible without numerically approximating the inverse.

The paper also derives a trace identity linking the Green operator to the dynamical zeta function—a generating function that packages all periodic orbits—and studies how a small Laplacian perturbation changes the gap between the origin and the rest of the landscape.

The Spectral Content of a Sagging String

A string’s resting shape and its vibration modes may look like separate stories. This paper shows how one contains a precise description of the other.

Take a string fixed at both ends and loaded uniformly. At rest it sags into a smooth curve—the torsion function for the interval. When plucked, it vibrates in modes described by the Dirichlet Laplacian. We show that the resting curve can be divided into local quadratic profiles aligned with the nodal intervals of those modes. The reciprocal profile identifies the unique extremum of the corresponding mode.

The resting string\(-u''(x)=1,\quad u(0)=u(L)=0\)The uniformly loaded string is the Dirichlet torsion function on an interval.

A global spectral expansion then expresses the torsion function as a uniformly convergent sum of its odd eigenfunctions. In this one-dimensional setting, equilibrium shape, nodal structure, and vibration fit into a single picture.

For a fixed-end string, the eigenfunctions divide the interval into nodal pieces. The paper shows that the torsion profile—the curve formed by the uniformly loaded, resting string—has a corresponding local quadratic structure on those very pieces. Its local shape and the mode’s extremum are related through reciprocation.

These local identities are assembled into a global expansion over the odd eigenfunctions. The result gives a full one-dimensional account of how a static equilibrium profile reflects spectral information, a setting closely related in spirit to landscape methods that use static functions to understand spectra.

Fractal Geometry & Brownian Motion

Real analysis and measure theory led to a first independent paper on how to measure shapes that have no ordinary length.

After completing my school’s four-year high-school mathematics curriculum in eighteen months, I looked for ways to continue beyond the classroom. I applied to Euler Circle’s Cryptography 2024 and Independent Problem Research Workshop (IPRW 2024) while in tenth grade.

A proof snippet · Brownian motion\(\mathbb{E}[I_\alpha(\mu_B)]<\infty,\quad 0<\alpha<2\)Finite \(\alpha\)-energy yields \(\dim_H(\operatorname{Range} B)\geq\alpha\); let \(\alpha\uparrow2\).

I began with real analysis under Ari Krishna, working through selected chapters of Princeton Lectures in Analysis alongside An Infinitely Large Napkin. From there I moved into measure theory: Lebesgue measure, Borel σ-algebras, measurable functions, and introductory stochastic processes. Those ideas gave me the tools for my first independent research project.

My paper, Unveiling Fractal Geometry through the Hausdorff Dimension, explored self-similarity, box-counting dimension, and the more flexible Hausdorff measure. The central idea is to cover a complicated set at smaller and smaller scales and ask how the total weighted size of those covers behaves. This lets us describe sets whose dimension is not an integer. In the paper, I also use the energy method on Brownian motion: for each \(0<\alpha<2\), the expected \(\alpha\)-energy of its occupation measure is finite. This implies the path’s Hausdorff dimension is at least every \(\alpha<2\), and hence reaches 2.

I later presented this work to more than thirty Euler Circle students, teaching assistants, and faculty. Euler Circle’s research community has remained a part of my mathematical life since then.
